The Scale of the Crisis

How Big Is Uganda's Kidney Crisis?

Kidney disease is largely silent — patients often have no symptoms until more than half their kidney function is already lost. HIV amplifies the burden substantially among Uganda's 1.4 million people living with the virus. The numbers below give a sense of the scale. The case study that follows gives it a face.

2–7%
General population with kidney disease
Between 2 and 7 in every hundred Ugandans have some form of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Among people living with HIV — roughly 1.4 million Ugandans — the rate rises to approximately 15 in every hundred. APOL1 high-risk variants prevalent in East African populations accelerate this HIV-CKD pathway.
PMC9438417 · Global Dialysis Perspective: Uganda
12.5%
CKD prevalence in eastern Uganda
Eastern Uganda carries more than three times the kidney disease burden of southwestern Uganda (3.9%). A difference this large — within a single country — cannot be explained away. It demands district-level investigation. The ROYGBIV atlas maps this gradient in real time.
Muiru et al. · SEARCH-CKD study · PLOS One 2020
134+
Districts with no published kidney data
Of Uganda's 146 administrative districts, fewer than twelve have any published kidney disease data. The remaining 134 — home to tens of millions of people — are a complete blank on the scientific map. This is the gap we are closing, starting now.
Ukubona Atlas Landscape Survey · 2026 · Updated per Basaza correction
17%
HIV prevalence among people who inject drugs
PWID in Uganda carry HIV prevalence roughly three times the national adult rate of 6%. This population is the target of Boniface's AI-IBBS Track 2 application (PAR-24-329). HIV in this group is also a direct driver of CKD via HIVAN and immunological inflammation, linking both tracks biologically.
Mudiope et al. · PLOS Global Public Health 2025 · Harm Reduction International 2025
≈ 0
Domestic kidney transplants currently active
Uganda's first domestic kidney transplants were performed at Mulago in 2023 — fewer than ten procedures before suspension in 2024 pending regulatory framework. Every patient who needs a transplant is currently referred abroad. The Atlas makes the demand visible before it arrives at Mulago.
Mulago pilot suspended 2024 · Monitor Uganda
$30–55K
Cost of a kidney transplant abroad
A kidney transplant in India costs $25,000–$35,000. Emergency cases reach $55,000 — the cost that arrived in Prof. Basaza's inbox the same night he corrected the district count. A domestic programme could deliver the same for ~$8,000. The economic case is overwhelming.
Africanews · Uganda Parliament debate 2022 · Live case, March 2026

What Drives Kidney Disease in Uganda

Risk Factors: A Profile
Unlike the West

The causes of CKD in Uganda are not identical to those in high-income countries. HIV infection is a major driver. APOL1 high-risk genotypes — common in East African populations — interact with HIV to accelerate nephropathy. The most striking finding from SEARCH-CKD is how many cases have no identifiable risk factor at all. That unknown column alone justifies a 146-district investigation.

Two thirds of CKD cases in rural East Africa have no identifiable risk factor from the standard clinical checklist. This is not a data quality problem. It strongly suggests Uganda-specific drivers — including APOL1 × HIV interaction — are responsible for a substantial share of the burden. These hypotheses are entirely unstudied at district level.

Boniface's AI-IBBS system (Track 2) maps the HIV risk networks that feed into this burden — hotspots of transmission, high-risk individuals, gaps in care. The CKD Atlas (Track 1) maps the downstream organ damage. Together they describe the full causal chain: from behaviour to biology to broken kidney to $55,000 flight to India.

The Transplant Crisis

Sixty Years with No
Kidney Transplant Programme

Uganda has never established a sustained kidney transplant programme. For six decades, patients have gone to India, Kenya, or Turkey. The timeline below traces this history — and where the Ukubona × UCU collaboration sits within it.

1960s – 1990s
Uganda gains independence but inherits almost no kidney care infrastructure. Ugandan patients begin making the journey to Kenyatta National Hospital, Nairobi.
2000s – 2010s
India emerges as primary destination. Cost: $25,000–$35,000. As of 2012, Uganda had just two dialysis chairs and two specialist nephrologists nationally. HIV epidemic expands CKD burden through HIVAN.
2020
Parliament passes Human Organ Donation and Tissue Transplant Bill. Implementation stalled pending Presidential assent.
2022
Dialysis capacity: 94 chairs and 14 nephrologists nationally. Parliament records: domestic transplant ~$8,000 versus $30,000 abroad. The equity argument is on the parliamentary record.
December 2023 – 2024
Mulago performs Uganda's first kidney transplants — approximately 4–10 procedures. Programme suspended while MoH develops regulatory framework. Patients return to medical tourism queue.

How This Project Works

A Living Map, Not
a One-Off Survey

The atlas is a living platform that improves with each research cycle. CKD is the first layer. HIV burden, PWUS hotspots, and mental health prevalence follow. The dropdown is the interface. The slope of the manifold is the policy signal.

We begin with what the published literature already tells us — patchy, concentrated in a handful of districts, but sufficient to establish a starting point. UCU students close those gaps systematically, district by district. Each student cohort generates real data and a citable publication.

The AI-IBBS system (Track 2) generates real-time behavioural surveillance data on HIV risk networks. As that data matures, it populates a second dropdown layer on the atlas — HIV transmission hotspots by district, linkable to downstream CKD risk.

Once CKD and HIV layers are established, the same infrastructure maps mental health, maternal mortality, hypertension, and diabetes — positioning UCU as Uganda's leading institution for district-level public health intelligence.
